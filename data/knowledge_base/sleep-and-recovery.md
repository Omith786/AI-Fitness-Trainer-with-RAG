---
title: Sleep, Rest and Recovery
source: Curated fitness knowledge base (original text)

Training provides the stimulus for improvement, but the actual adaptation happens during recovery. Muscles repair, the nervous system recovers and energy stores are replenished between sessions, which is why rest is a part of training rather than a break from it.

Sleep is the single most important recovery tool. Most adults function and recover best with seven to nine hours of good-quality sleep per night. Chronic short sleep is associated with reduced strength and endurance performance, greater perceived effort, impaired appetite regulation and slower recovery. Consistent sleep and wake times, a dark cool room, and limiting screens and caffeine late in the day all support better sleep.

Rest days let accumulated fatigue dissipate. For most people training hard several times a week, one to three rest or light days per week is appropriate. Rest does not have to mean doing nothing: gentle activity such as walking, easy cycling or mobility work, sometimes called active recovery, can aid circulation and help you feel better without adding meaningful fatigue.

Warning signs of inadequate recovery include persistent fatigue, declining performance, disturbed sleep, low mood, elevated resting heart rate and nagging aches. If these appear, reducing training volume or intensity for a week is usually more productive than pushing harder.

Nutrition and hydration also support recovery. Eating enough total energy and protein, and drinking enough fluid, give your body the raw materials it needs to adapt to the training you have done.

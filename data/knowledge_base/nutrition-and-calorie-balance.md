Body weight change is governed largely by energy balance: the relationship between the calories you consume and the calories you expend. A sustained energy deficit leads to weight loss, a surplus leads to weight gain, and balance maintains weight. The type of food still matters for health, hunger and performance, but the overall balance sets the direction.

To lose fat at a sustainable rate, a modest deficit of roughly 300 to 500 calories per day is a reasonable starting point for many people, producing gradual loss of around a quarter to half a kilogram per week. Aggressive deficits often backfire through excessive hunger, muscle loss and difficulty adhering to the plan.

To gain muscle, a small surplus of around 200 to 400 calories per day, combined with resistance training and adequate protein, supports lean gains while limiting unnecessary fat gain. Very large surpluses add fat faster than muscle.

The quality of the diet influences how easy the balance is to maintain. Meals rich in protein, vegetables, fruit, whole grains and legumes are filling and nutrient dense, which helps control appetite. Highly processed foods can be included in moderation but are easy to overconsume.

Rather than counting calories forever, many people use it as a temporary learning tool to understand portion sizes, then transition to simpler habits such as building balanced plates and eating to consistent hunger and fullness cues.

Individual needs vary with age, sex, body size, activity and health conditions. These figures are general starting points, not prescriptions, and anyone with a medical condition should seek personalised advice.
